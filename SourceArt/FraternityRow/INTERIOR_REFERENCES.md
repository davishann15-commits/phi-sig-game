# Fraternity Row Interior References

Research date: 2026-10-18. Target scene: October 1986, Homecoming night, dusk into darkness.

Real organizations and buildings identify reference sources only. Do not reproduce their names, letters, crests, portrait photographs, or other artwork in the game. Build original geometry, markings, and fictional composite grids.

## Evidence labels and limits

- **Photo observation:** directly visible in a photograph inspected during this research.
- **Documented:** explicitly described by the institution, chapter, architectural archive, or historic nomination.
- **Adaptation:** proposed game design, not proof that every fraternity house contained a feature in 1986.
- Contemporary photographs establish room use, spatial relationships, and accumulated wear. Their modern appliances, electronics, lighting, and renovations are not period references.
- Basement references below explain real chapter-house planning. The current Row proposal uses ground-floor chapter rooms and does not excavate basements or alter frozen terrain.

## 1. Colonial Revival: 1932 chapter house, University of Idaho

[National Register nomination, section 7, pages 2–3](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/4ee6c7a0-02b8-4424-96ab-9631714468c9)

**Documented:** Social rooms flank the entry hall and central stair. The basement contains dining, kitchen, recreation, and service spaces. Its dining room retains exposed brick; the commercial kitchen combines stainless fixtures with older wooden cabinets. The knotty-pine pool room received conversation pits around the 1960s. A formal-room fireplace is flanked by a trophy case and cabinets.

**Period evidence:** The nomination dates study-room and shared-bathroom alterations to 1978 and lounge carpeting to 1983–84. It also records dark sheet paneling, acoustical ceiling tile, and older woodwork beneath newer finishes.

**Adaptation:** Use layers of renovation: formal Colonial circulation, retained French doors, worn carpet, paneling, and a practical rear kitchen. This house uses communal sleeping rooms, so it does not support treating small doubles as universal.

## 2. Tudor Revival: 1931 chapter house, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute

[National Register nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/badba067-88d6-4c0e-95cd-d7a51328af46/)

**Documented:** Brick construction, steep slate gables, half-timbered entrance pavilion, metal casement windows, and a hillside basement. The small vestibule has slate flooring, molded oak trim, and a Tudor-arched door surround. Recessed windows sit in thick plaster walls. A larger hall distributes circulation toward living and dining areas.

**Adaptation:** A compact slate vestibule opens into an oak-and-plaster hall; use recessed casements and a heavy fireplace. Dining and kitchen remain adjacent toward the rear. Carry the Tudor character inside instead of putting a generic white corridor behind its facade.

**Access note:** The nomination's indexed text was retrievable; its direct PDF viewer was intermittent during research. These are documented text observations, not a claim that all its photographs were inspected.

## 3. Queen Anne converted residence: Edward Wells House, University of Vermont

[UVM: A Home for Alumni, December 2010](https://www.uvm.edu/uvmnews/news/home-alumni)

**Documented:** UVM identifies this as Queen Anne architecture and describes its long fraternity occupancy. The first-floor entry hall has golden-oak paneling, a large fireplace on red marble, and flanking benches. The library has a ceiling formed from cased beams; ornate interior woodcarving survives throughout the house.

**Adaptation:** Give the compact Victorian a distinct entry/stair hall, connected parlors, and a turret reading alcove within its approved body envelope. Retained fine woodwork should coexist with secondhand couches, cheap student shelving, and worn rugs.

**Limit:** This is a large mansion precedent. Borrow its room hierarchy and details, not its footprint or lavish finish everywhere.

## 4. Modern chapter house: Paul Rudolph, Auburn, 1961–63

[Paul Rudolph Institute architectural archive](https://prhf-a9m5.squarespace.com/196102-kappa-sigma-fraternity-house)

**Documented:** The archive records concrete-and-brick construction, two-person sleeping rooms, royal-blue carpet, a green den, and a paprika-colored dining hall entered through double swinging doors. Dining, kitchen, and bath were exceptions to otherwise extensive carpeting.

**Photo observations:** Completed-project photographs show painted masonry, plain flat-roofed massing, deep recesses, a timber stair with dark metal connections, and a blue-carpeted landing with a simple upholstered bench.

## 5. Lived-in rooms and basement: Drexel chapter's published tour

## 6. Composite stairwell, lounge, bar, and dining: MIT chapter first floor

[Published first-floor tour](https://pkt.mit.edu/house/first/)

**Documented:** A lounge contains a fireplace, pool/foosball activities, and a bar. A separate dining area doubles as study space. The main stair runs through the building, with composites dating from the late 1960s onward lining its walls.

**Adaptation:** Place fictional composite grids by year along a stair and hallway rather than repeating identical pictures everywhere. Dining furniture can serve meetings and studying. A bar is a plausible social-space component, not proof every house needs one.

**Period limit:** The page explicitly describes a smart television; use a freestanding CRT and period equipment instead.

## 7. Chapter-room reuse, shared bathroom, and lofted beds: MIT second floor

[Published second-floor tour](https://pkt.mit.edu/house/second/)

**Documented:** The chapter room retains original wood paneling, couch seating, and award plaques, and supports meetings, piano practice, studying, calls, and occasional guests. Lofted beds stand over desks in a large converted room. There is a bathroom on each floor.

**Adaptation:** A chapter room needs a readable meeting arrangement but can still contain ordinary student life. Optional upper-floor glimpses can show crowded beds/desks, storage, and a shared bath rather than hotel suites.

**Period limit:** The present arrangement does not establish lofted doubles in 1986. Its bathroom mural anecdote concerns the 1987 pledge class and must not be copied into this 1986 scene.

## Recurring spatial lessons

1. Entry halls and stairs establish house identity.
2. Formal spaces and ordinary communal spaces overlap.
3. Dining and kitchen have a practical relationship.
4. Composites, plaques, and trophies accumulate across years.
5. Student furniture comes from different generations rather than a matched showroom set.
6. Service spaces contain pipes, storage, supplies, and durable surfaces.
7. Older architectural fabric survives beneath later paneling, carpet, and repairs.
8. Shared bedrooms and baths imply visible storage pressure.

## October 1986 dressing decisions

These are proposed art choices, not claims that all sources show them:

- Freestanding woodgrain CRT, large speakers, cassette deck/receiver, and record sleeves.
- Hall rotary or coin telephone with cord, note pad, and handwritten numbers. The interrupted-party house can leave its receiver off the hook.
- Worn commercial carpet in busy rooms; shag only as an older leftover in a lounge.
- Paneling, mismatched lamps, ashtrays, fictional pennants, typed notices, and handwritten house rosters.
- Existing lower-floor references can inform exposed-service ceilings or aging acoustical tile in utility/chapter rooms; they do not require new underground rooms.
- Party houses: furniture shifted aside, isolated spill marks, food left on tables, cups clustered where people gathered, and gear left ready to play.
- Quiet houses: orderly chairs, unobstructed tables, restrained objects, and deliberate lighting.
- No modern displays, visible LED fixtures, QR codes, contemporary posters, or dates after October 1986.
- No human portraits copied from source composites; build original grids through the project's own geometry/material system.
- Preserve the shared interior real-light pool at six. Other lamps, windows, and equipment glow remain emissive.

## Fictional Greek identities

Two- and three-letter combinations frequently belong to real social, professional, honorary, or local organizations. Provisional four-letter fictional combinations are:

- **ΩΞΛΗ** — Omega Xi Lambda Eta
- **ΨΗΞΡ** — Psi Eta Xi Rho
- **ΗΞΩΡ** — Eta Xi Omega Rho
- **ΛΩΗΞ** — Lambda Omega Eta Xi
- **ΡΞΗΨ** — Rho Xi Eta Psi

These are proposed fiction, not certified unused names. A limited search did not establish an exact organization match for the first three full names; that is not an exhaustive registry check. Keep all four letters together and avoid shortening to a real organization's familiar abbreviation. Create original crests rather than adapting real heraldry. Final assignments belong in the reviewed Row plan.
